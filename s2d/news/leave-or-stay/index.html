The decision no one signs

Leave or stay: for a Cuban ballplayer, both answers cost the life he had. The players who made the choice, in one direction and the other, tell what it cost them.

In Cuba, the biggest decision of a ballplayer's life isn't signed in any office.

There's no contract, no agent, no lawyer. It's made at night, in a hotel, in the woods, on a boat, or at the kitchen table looking at your mother. And both possible answers cost the same: the life you had.

This is the story of that decision, told by the men who made it one way and the other.

Because on this site we've told the stories of those who left and those who stayed, and when you put them side by side something emerges that nobody says out loud: there was no good choice. There were two punishments, and each man picked his own.

What they offered you

Let's start by dismantling an idea: that everyone was offered millions. No. Real offers reached only the elite, and they arrived in secret.

A scout slipped into Antonio Scull's hotel at night with a briefcase: fifty thousand dollars in hand and two million waiting. Lázaro Junco was passed a check tucked inside a magazine. An Orioles scout sought out Gabriel Pierre by name and told him the whole thing in one sentence: “you're going to be a millionaire.”

All three said no at that moment. And all three paid anyway.

Because in Cuba the offer wasn't a prize: it was a test. If they made you one, someone in the system already knew about it.

What leaving cost

The one who left lost everything at once: his family, his name, his stadium and his country. They erased you from the statistics, took you off television and, for years, you couldn't even go back to bury your own.

Rey Vicente Anglada, who managed Team Cuba, sums it up in a figure nobody wants to hear: of those who sign, only about three percent establish themselves in the major leagues. Agent Carlos Pérez puts it in another ratio: getting there and staying there is thirty percent quality and seventy percent luck.

Yordanys Pérez left by boat. They gave him eight at-bats in Triple-A and cut him loose. That's leaving too.

And the ones who did make it didn't arrive whole either. The ones who triumphed abroad played knowing that in their own country they no longer existed.

Leaving wasn't winning. It was betting your whole life on a coin that came up right three times out of a hundred.

What staying cost

The one who stayed thought he was buying peace. He was buying something else.

Braudilio Vinent, the Meteor of La Maya, said no to the scouts and dedicated every win to the Revolution. Today, at seventy, he shows his medals to the camera so someone will remember he's alive.

Gabriel Pierre, the one the Orioles came for, wasn't punished for the offer. He was summoned to a State Security office for going with his wife to study the Bible.

Carlos Tabares was benched for five years without being told why. Eduardo Paret lost four over a phone call, without a trial, and he still doesn't know who gave the order. Juan Carlos Millán was promised a car in 1992 that never arrived.

Agustín Marquetti, the man behind the most famous hit in Cuban baseball, used to ride home from the stadium on a bicycle. Today he says it bluntly:

“If I were in Cuba, I'd have been dead nine years ago.”

And Hernán Valdés did the hardest thing of all: he went back. He returned to Cuba for baseball, and it took him forty-nine years to be able to leave again. His summary fits in a single phrase: “They deceived us.”

Staying wasn't winning either. It was handing over your career in exchange for a promise nobody intended to keep.

There is no “what if”

This is where the fan comes in with the usual line: “if he had left, he would have been a star.” Or the opposite: “that guy wouldn't have made it in his dreams.”

Both are the same mistake. In baseball, what isn't played isn't data: it's wishful thinking.

Tabares says it better than anyone. He had opportunities from '98 on. When he was twenty-two his father had a stroke; he came back to Cuba and they never let him leave again. He admits himself that maybe he would have been great, or maybe he wouldn't have been any good. But one thing he's clear on:

Sign? I was going to sign.

That's why at Recta we don't say Tabares would have made it. We say nobody can claim one thing or the other. Sometimes you don't try because you can't, not because you don't want to: in Cuba, leaving meant abandoning your family, being erased and, if they caught you, being sanctioned.

Why nobody signs it

There's a deeper reason this decision comes with no paperwork, and it's worth spelling out in full.

A decision gets signed when both options are legal. When one of them makes you a traitor by law, there's nothing to sign: there's an emergency exit and a wall.

That's why it makes no sense to judge the one who left or the one who stayed. Both decided with a gun on the table that neither of them put there. The man on the boat and the man on the bicycle are the same ballplayer on two different nights.

In Cuba they were all victims. And someone who can't choose doesn't decide: he survives.

So the next time someone says “that guy sold out” or “that guy stayed because he was a coward,” ask them just one thing: which of the two would you have signed, if neither one could be signed?

The statements quoted are taken verbatim from Recta Al Pecho's interviews with Antonio Scull, Gabriel Pierre, Braudilio Vinent, Carlos Tabares, Eduardo Paret, Juan Carlos Millán, Agustín Marquetti, Hernán Valdés, Yordanys Pérez and Rey Vicente Anglada, available in full on our YouTube channel.
