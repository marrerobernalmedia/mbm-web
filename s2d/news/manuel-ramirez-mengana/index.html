Mengana: engineer or ballplayer

Manuel Ramírez "Mengana" faced Armando Capiró as a teenager and hit Team Cuba's pitchers. At 22 a professor made him choose between the Serie Nacional and engineering. He did the math.

The interview opens with a memory from the bleachers. In October 1984, during the Baseball World Cup in Havana, Manuel Ramírez "Mengana" left the university buried in work and went to the Latinoamericano stadium to clear his head. He sat in the nearly empty right-field stands, and in his first at-bat a left-handed hitter from the United States, "dark-skinned, tall, thin," lined a ball that arrived with frightening violence near his seat. He didn't know the kid's name. Years later he did: Barry Bonds.

Santiago, Tony Taylor and a first fly ball

Mengana is from Santiago de Cuba, and baseball ran in the family. An uncle, a catcher, signed with the Boston organization at 18 and played in the minor leagues until a pitch broke his arm. His home had a television in 1957, rare in his neighborhood, and that is how he started watching games. His favorite player was Tony Taylor, the Phillies second baseman, who he says was the only one who crossed himself before stepping into the box.

He moved to Havana at 10, in 1961. On his school's field he borrowed a lefty's glove, went out to the outfield and caught his first fly ball without anyone ever teaching him. Then came the sports scholarship, the school games and coaches who had played professionally: "By 15 we had the fundamentals down."

"Keep it up, kid"

At 15, younger than allowed, he played in the provincial junior league against Armando Capiró and Rodolfo Puente, and by his account hit over .400. In one of those games Ramón Carneado saw him, the manager who won four championships with Industriales and who, Mengana says, was never given Team Cuba because he did not share the ideology of those years. Carneado pulled him aside: "Keep it up, kid."

In the top amateur division every Team Cuba player took part, and Mengana faced those pitchers. Asked which one gave him the most trouble, he answers: "None of them." In his head, the good pitchers were the ones he had to hit to prove he belonged at another level. Everto Blanco, who later managed his team, summed it up: "You hit good pitching."

"Engineer or ballplayer"

By 22 he had made three tries at reaching the Serie Nacional, Cuba's top league, and was never picked. "Not for discipline, not for any problem," he says; maybe because he wasn't one to smile easily. Then a professor took him aside and made him choose: engineer or ballplayer.

Mengana did the math. As he tells it, Capiró, the cleanup hitter for Team Cuba, earned 128 pesos; as an engineer he would earn 340. Very few players back then had more than a middle-school education. He lived alone with his mother, a former housekeeper who dreamed of seeing him become a professional. He left baseball and went to college. "Baseball left my hands, but it never left my mind."

His son's numbers

The bug came back when his son, shortstop Roberto Carlos Ramírez, reached the Serie Nacional. When Roberto Carlos became an everyday player with the Metropolitanos, his father charted every at-bat and told him to make the most of it: he was fast, a switch-hitter and could bunt to any angle. He finished that season at .340 with 126 hits.

Wood, aluminum and Double-A

For Mengana, the best era of the Serie Nacional runs from 1962 to 1976, with wooden bats, and above all the 1970s. He knows aluminum firsthand: after 15 years away, at 39 or 40, he won the RBI title in a provincial veterans league swinging metal. He busts the myth that the ball carried less in the 1980s: from 1962 to 1976, fewer than ten players a year hit .300, and with aluminum the list tripled. In his view, Cuba's 1977 provincial redistricting and aluminum bats arrived together and spread the pitching thin across many more teams, which is why, as a whole, he doesn't think the Serie Nacional reaches Double-A: "Double-A is strong. People are wrong about that."

Víctor Mesa and Silvio Montejo

He has an opinion he says he has repeated online: Víctor Mesa was not a five-tool player but a four-tool one. "The fifth one he didn't have: arm strength." And he ranks Silvio Montejo, from the same province, above him, with a stronger arm and better range.

Mengana's story is that of a ballplayer good enough for the Serie Nacional who, when he had to choose, did what an engineer does: he ran the numbers. Half a century later, he is still doing it, now with baseball's numbers.
